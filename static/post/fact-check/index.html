Fact check status: True

I genuinely sent this to Gina Casalegno, CMU's Dean of Students and Vice President for Student Affairs. This is a direct copy of the relevant SMTP headers.

Hi Gina,

I was sitting for about 20 minutes tonight waiting for the train. I was in long sleeves and it wasn't that cold. But I'm sick right now (from an ordinary cold), and I was so tired I could barely think under the brain fog, and I'm so thin now that all the cold made it to my bones before the train finally came up to the platform.

I was sitting for about 20 minutes tonight waiting for the train. I was in long sleeves and it wasn't that cold.

Fact check status: True

I was taking the T northbound from the south hills to downtown Pittsburgh, probably close to midnight. I was wearing a long-sleeve pink top from Hollister, which is the most white-girl thing I own, and my typical long black pants. The temperature was around 10°C (40°F), plus or minus a couple degrees.

But I'm sick right now (from an ordinary cold), and I was so tired I could barely think under the brain fog, and I'm so thin now that all the cold made it to my bones before the train finally came up to the platform.

Fact check status: Mostly true

This is poetic liberty. I was getting over a cold at the time (in the runny-nose-and-slight-cough phase I think), and I was certainly tired and cold, but from a physiological standpoint it seems unlikely that the cold "made it to my bones" within 20 minutes.

I sat in the train feeling small in a way I never have in my life. Physically small, because I have lost so much weight, but also small in the universe. I felt an overwhelming wish that some force beyond my comprehension would lift me up as a cold, tired, sick little emaciated creature and cradle me in its arms and nurse me back to health, and set me back down, safe and comfortable and whole again. I cannot share this with anyone because I am the one who is strong and optimistic and powerful.

I sat in the train feeling small in a way I never have in my life. Physically small, because I have lost so much weight, but also small in the universe.

Fact check status: Mostly true

As I describe later in this article, I had lost a significant amount of weight in the months before this was written. While I was still six feet tall on a good day with dashing good looks, "physically small" in comparison with what I was used to is a reasonable description. Several people in the preceding days, including a friend, a family member, and a medical professional, had noted that my face looked visibly thinner than a few months beforehand, which I found distressing. To say that this is a way I have "never [felt] in my life" is a statement that is difficult to disprove, but probably accurate.

I felt an overwhelming wish that some force beyond my comprehension would lift me up as a cold, tired, sick little emaciated creature and cradle me in its arms and nurse me back to health, and set me back down, safe and comfortable and whole again.

Fact check status: True

I had never voluntarily went to a doctor before this moment, aside from the time I wrecked my motorcycle and broke my wrist. I'm usually pretty independent, and I'm not religious or spiritual, which is part of why it was so distressing to suddenly feel like my physical wellbeing was so far out of my control that I could only hope for some force beyond my comprehension to intervene and save me.

I cannot share this with anyone because I am the one who is strong and optimistic and powerful.
If I do make the mistake of updating my support network on how I'm actually doing, they offer physical resources or sympathy, both of which I'm not interested in taking from them. Those should go to people who feel just as desperate as I do, but who will not at some point in a few months be just as strong and alive as they once were…

Fact check status: False

This is not a productive mindset to bring to receiving support from others. My description of myself as "strong and optimistic and powerful," particularly "the one" who has those attributes, suggests that I apply standards to myself that I would never apply to others. My stubborn insistence on never showing pain might say something about my upbringing. I, fact-checker Rosalyn, think that might be why some people found this blog post so distressing — such a sudden and overwhelming admission of weakness could be worrying when coming from someone whose vulnerability they were completely unused to seeing.

If I do make the mistake of updating my support network on how I'm actually doing, they offer physical resources or sympathy, both of which I'm not interested in taking from them. Those should go to people who feel just as desperate as I do, but who will not at some point in a few months be just as strong and alive as they once were, as will be the case for me, because I have the resilience that comes from the most basic privilege. It makes me think of the kids at CMU (and we are all kids. I am a kid, my classmates are kids, my TAs are kids, we're all barely starting the parts of our lives that are our own) who have felt the same physical stress and weakness that I do right now, but without the purpose that I have.

…at some point in a few months be just as strong and alive as they once were, as will be the case for me, because I have the resilience that comes from the most basic privilege.

Fact check status: Mostly true

Work in progress. I'm doing more okay now :-)

It makes me think of the kids at CMU … who have felt the same physical stress and weakness that I do right now, but without the purpose that I have.

Fact check status: Rhetoric

This is a statement of pure opinion. I've felt a strong sense of purpose in my life since I was 17 or so. I see myself as having a duty to improve this place as much as I can ("this place" is sometimes the whole world, and sometimes the communities I surround myself with, and sometimes just one person). It gives me the strength to tolerate a lot of confusion and pain, and sometimes I can't imagine how someone who just wants to live a normal-sized life and make something for themself and the handful of people closest to them can manage to do it. Sometimes they don't, I guess.

… (and we are all kids. I am a kid, my classmates are kids, my TAs are kids, we're all barely starting the parts of our lives that are our own) …

Fact check status: Mostly true

I think how "grown up" I've felt over time would be a graph that correlates very little with my actual age. I moved out of my parents' house in high school to live with some friends of mine, most of whom had been kicked out of their parents' houses for being queer. I was 17-turning-18 while I secretly took classes for my motorcycle license, toured apartments, and worked double shifts as a cashier to save up money for household goods. I stashed hundreds of dollars of pots and pans and kitchenware under my bed for months, which I'd bought at 25% off with my Christmas bonus and store partner discount.

Coming to college was a strange experience after that, because suddenly I had no utility bills, no worrying about insurance coverage, no kitchen to clean. I was suddenly allowed to be much less adult again. But I think in the years that've passed, I've changed how I perceive "grown-up-ness," and I don't think it would be fair to describe the 18-year-old with an apartment and bills piling up as anything other than a kid who happens to have had more ambition and responsibility than most. (And once I finally conceded to being transgender at the age of 20, a lot more changed with how I think about growing up and figuring out one's identity once they "start the part of their life that's their own.")

I forgive you for what you are doing to me. You know that you are torturing me — that you are depriving me of food, that you are putting me under immense physical and mental stress, that you denied me a place to live, that you are denying me places to study for the classes I am trying to take. But I think you know I'll survive it. What I do not forgive you for is the people I think about who did not make it out of CMU. You have drowned students under crushing burdens, without securing the resources and support they needed to make it through that. During midterms and finals, it is hard to find food on campus, even as a student with a dining plan and full access to campus. When students are overwhelmed by stress, they cannot see CaPS without admitting to (or faking) immediate risk to themselves. When students are underwater in deadlines and pressure (pressure not rooted in any actual danger, just words on paper), they have crushingly limited options to reduce the pressure they're under (and if you're out of vouchers, you might have no options). I have watched every person I love at CMU be broken down to what will probably be the lowest lows they will ever experience.

I forgive you for what you are doing to me.

Fact check status: True

Forgiveness is pretty easy when you see the human under the position of power. I'm just not a spiteful person like that, even when I know I shouldn't like or trust someone, I usually drift back to seeing them in a positive light. I think Gina Casalegno is a coward, but I can understand cowardice and it's not my place to judge it.

On the way to checking the status of "[y]ou know that you are torturing me", let's break down the list that follows it.

… you are depriving me of food …

Fact check status: True

This is the most documented thing CMU has done to hurt me. I've never had fully stable access to food as a CMU student, just due to CMU's limited interest in making food accessible to its students, but I'll spend more time on the baseline state of food access on campus later. My specific, documented struggles with accessing food begin in May, 2026, when I returned from my suspension for my second summer of classes. On my literal first day back on campus, May 11, 2026, I wrote to CMU's Dining Services to describe a software issue which prevented me from buying Dinex, one of several currencies CMU offers for buying food on-campus, which you can typically bill directly to your student invoices (which works best with financial aid, since otherwise I'd have to buy food on my personal credit card and accrue interest until I got a financial aid refund, if I got one at all). The subject line was "Bill Me" option not working for adding Dinex, and the body was a straightforward description of the issue.

Four days later, I asked Kelsey Murphy from Student Support Resources to follow up with Dining Services, since I hadn't gotten a reply.

One week after the original email, I emailed Dining Services again, just to follow up. On the same day, Kelsey Murphy replied to my email to her, and we briefly discussed the issue in more detail. Rather than following up with Dining Services herself, she told me to call them.

On May 20th, nine days after my initial email, Dining Services replied, saying they hoped to have the software issue solved by "next week." In their own words, "[t]he recommendation for the time being is to deposit via credit card." Why didn't I think of that? It goes without saying that that part of Dining Services' reply was deeply useless and condescending, and it reflects CMU's passive assumption that every student is rich and has financial support from their parents. I replied the next day to thank them for the update, and also to point out the issue with their reply (and the broader inaccessibility of food on campus).

I followed up again on June 7th, which was 27 days after the original email, and 18 days after they said it'd be a week or so until the issue was fixed. On June 9th, they gave me $50 of Dinex for free while they continued working on the issue, which is nice but only a couple days' worth of food.

On June 25th, 45 days after my initial email, they gave me another $50 in free Dinex, and offered to bill me for Dinex via a manual solution that their IT team had found.

On June 30th, 50 days after my initial email and more than halfway through the summer semester, they were finally able to manually bill me for Dinex and deposit it into my account.

But by June 30th, my second major issue with access to food had arisen. On June 26th, 2026, I was given my interim campus access restriction, prohibiting me from being present on CMU property at any times except for scheduled class meetings, with a 10 minute grace period before and after for traveling through campus. After that point, I was no longer able to eat food on campus, unless I strategically placed an order online and got lucky with the timing so that I could pick it up on the way into or out of class. Doing that on the way into class depends on it being a class in a space I was allowed to eat in (which is not true of the computer clusters, for example), and doing it on the way out of class depends on the weather being good enough that I can eat outside just outside the perimeter of campus. Due to class scheduling, that strategy was also only viable for 1–2 meals per day, and only on some days of the week.

On the same day that I was given my interim campus ban, I sent a partial appeal to Gina Casalegno, who was named as the only person I could appeal the decision to. My initial requests were for exceptions to be able to access the campus food pantry (which I had never used, but figured I should secure my access to), University Health Services, Counseling and Psychological Services, and the various offices I was already in communication with.

On June 29th, Gina Casalegno replied, deferring most of the outcomes of the appeal to Kelsey Murphy from Student Support Resources. I met with Kelsey Murphy on July 1st, where we discussed my food insecurity, housing insecurity, and other issues stemming from my campus access ban. My main request on the food security front was permission to go onto CMU property for a half-hour or so at meal times, so that I could buy food using the Dinex I'd finally managed to acquire. She took notes and stated she'd follow up with Gina Casalegno.

On July 6th, Gina Casalegno communicated the outcomes of the meeting to me. In her words, "your request to dine on campus is not in keeping with the decision to restrict your access to campus under the SIP [Safety Intervention Protocol, the technical name for the interim campus access restriction]."

Throughout the summer, I routinely ate a fraction of my usual dietary intake. Once the fall semester started, my schedule became even less compatible with accessing food on campus with the 10 minute grace period trick, and I've given up trying. I rarely get more than one full meal in a day. I have continued to plead with CMU for access to campus so I can have reliable access to food. I have experienced significant negative health effects from my limited access to food, which I'll describe in more detail later on. CMU has absolutely knowingly and willingly deprived me of food.

… you are putting me under immense physical and mental stress …

Fact check status: True

As I've documented and will document throughout the rest of this post, my physical health has deteriorated as a result of CMU's actions, and I've also been under constant stress as a result of the "free Palestine by any means necessary" Fence investigation looming over me. For months on end, my entire future has been uncertain. For several of those months, the situation was dynamic enough that I was constantly in fight-or-flight, worried that an email at any point in time could change the outcome of the hearing or further destabilize my living situation. I've discussed this with multiple psychologists, both in and out of CMU, and they could attest to the harm that I've experienced from the stress CMU has put me under.

What I do not forgive you for is the people I think about who did not make it out of CMU.

Fact check status: Mostly true

I might forgive the human buried under the position of power, but those two cannot be fully separated. I do not forgive the Dean of Students, the Vice President for Student Affairs, for the harm she has done to my community. I believe forgiveness is immensely powerful — you can't have accountability without forgiveness, and without accountability, no community can process harm in a positive way. But Gina Casalegno, the Dean of Students, the Vice President for Student Affairs, has no interest in accountability, and so for the time being, I have no right to forgive her for the harm she has done to others in my community. It's not like my opinion would typically affect her, but part of my goal in framing the thoughts I sent to Gina around the concept of forgiveness was to make it clear that whether or not the university is willing to think of me as human when it hurts me, I still see individual people making decisions, not the abstraction of "the university." It's one thing to have a faceless system hurting you, it's another thing entirely to be able to look into the eyes of who's doing it.
