CMU is using food insecurity as a negotiating tactic

UNPUBLISHED · UNPUBLISHED · UNPUBLISHED

On June 9th, 2026, I painted the phrase "free Palestine by any means necessary" on The Fence, a structure on CMU's campus that students are allowed to paint after midnight. In the following weeks, a furious back-and-forth took place, with numerous pro-Palestinian Fence paintings being met with condemnation and censorship by CMU's upper administration. The university used its sworn police force to intimidate painters, and 10 of us were contacted for questioning. CMU opened investigations against 9 students alleging that the paintings violated the Discriminatory and Sexual Misconduct Policy, by supposedly creating a hostile environment for Jewish and Israeli students, as defined by Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

CMU ultimately resolved 8 of the 9 cases against students with no finding of responsibility, though not before comparing its own students (some of whom were themselves Jewish) to the perpetrator of the 2018 Tree of Life synagogue mass shooting, which happened in the same neighborhood as CMU's campus. The university is under significant political and financial pressure to avoid association with the pro-Palestinian cause, and its upper administration has shown its willingness to fight dirty. I'm the one student who is still under investigation. If I am found responsible in my upcoming hearing, I will likely be forced to separate from CMU, which I consider my home and my community.

CMU has continuously put pressure on me to accept an alternative resolution (basically a plea deal) which would require me to separate from CMU — a completely unacceptable outcome — without it having to prove its case in a hearing. In order to pressure me to accept it, the university has made use of its "Safety Intervention Protocol" — intended to be used when the subject of an investigation poses a threat to the safety of the campus community — to issue an interim campus access restriction. Since June, I've been banned from campus property at any time other than my scheduled classes, under threat of arrest. The university has documentation from a forensic psychologist that I do not post a threat to the campus community, and it has already released 8 other students from similar restrictions for the same disciplinary charges. But by invoking the Safety Intervention Protocol, it can deny my access to food, housing, healthcare, and a fair opportunity to utilize the academic resources made available to students outside of class hours. My goal with this post is to show that CMU's actions have in fact limited my access to food, that it knows that they've done so, and that it is willing to use my resulting state of food insecurity to pressure me into accepting its proposed resolution.

background

I already had an off-and-on history of food insecurity on CMU's campus before the investigation started. During my time as a student, I've been largely reliant on CMU's on-campus dining halls, restaurants, and grocery store, as most students are (unless they have a full kitchen in their dorm or off-campus residence, and sufficient time to cook). Unfortunately, the hours and reliability of those on-campus food sources are limited, resulting in spotty access to food during breaks, final exam weeks, or late at night. In the months leading up to the pro-Palestine Fence paintings that would provoke the investigation, my access to on-campus food was also limited by a payment-related software bug.

CMU splits the summer into two 6-week semesters: "summer 1" and "summer 2." The first day of summer 1 classes was May 11th, 2026. Right on that first day, I wrote to CMU's Dining Services to describe a software issue which prevented me from buying Dinex, one of several currencies CMU offers for buying food on-campus. One can typically bill Dinex directly to their student invoice, which works best with financial aid, since the alternative is to buy food on one's personal credit card and accrue interest until they receive some future financial aid refund. I'm simultaneously receiving funds from CMU's institutional financial aid and transferred post-9/11 GI bill from my father, so it's difficult to guess how those will interact and predict how much I'll be refunded for living expenses. But the "bill me" option wasn't working, preventing me from buying it unless I used my personal credit card (presenting the same issue as just buying food with my credit card directly). pt ii. food insecurity

For a plane to crash, more than one thing typically has to go wrong. Likewise, for someone to go without sufficient food in a prosperous country as a student of one of its 20 highest regarded colleges or universities, more than one factor has to limit their nutritional intake.

I think it's useful to break down the set of things that need to go right in order for someone in our context to secure an adequate nutritional intake:

  • They need to have access to food, whether with money or through support from others
  • They need to physically go to the grocery store or restaurant to buy food
  • They need time to cook and/or prepare some foods
  • They need a functioning kitchen to safely store, refrigerate, and/or cook some foods
  • They need foods that are compatible with any dietary restrictions they may have
  • They need food in a sufficient amount and with sufficient variety to fulfill nutritional requirements
  • Their body needs to be able to physically take in and process food in sufficient amounts

Over the last six months, I have had something go wrong with any one of the above factors at some point in time. At any moment, I was unable to secure some handful of those things, and as a result, not taking in as much food as I need to operate at full capacity. I think it's important to start by making that clear, because it's very easy to latch onto one of those factors and identify a way someone could take some "personal responsibility" and fix it for themself. My simplest argument against that sort of victim blaming is that I don't want to be hungry. Full stop. It sucks waking up fatigued, dragging yourself through a day of brain fog, losing muscle mass you worked hard to build up, constantly worrying about a basic human survival need.

Sometimes, one of the limitations to my food security has been insufficient resources. My financial stability is dependent on CMU — I don't have time to work a part-time job as a full-time student focusing on one of the most rigorous subjects at one of the most rigorous colleges in the country. During stretches where financial aid isn't financially aiding, sometimes the barrier really is just my card declining. But most of the time, that hasn't been the case, and if it were, it'd be one of the easier problems to fix. I have a lot of friends who'd gladly give me more shelf-stable food than I could carry home, and CMU has shoved the fact that it has a food pantry down my throat for months as if it's a cure-all for their liability. That said, money also has a softer sort of chilling effect. Even if I can afford food right now, if I'm already constrained by other factors, it's easy for the guilt of another charge on my bank balance to tip the scales toward going to sleep a little hungry.

More commonly, a major limitation is having to physically go to the food. Part of why I've made such a big deal out of on-campus dining halls and restaurants is that those are by far the fastest and lowest-friction ways to access food during the school day or a long study session. "Time poverty" is absolutely a thing — having a full course load spanning four unrelated subjects, on top of other responsibilities, chores, rest, and a bare-minimum of socialization makes time precious. When you have a quiz coming up in an hour, and you're hungry, and you don't have a convenient source of food, choosing between spending that hour on last-minute studying vs. having breakfast that day is a tough choice. My current apartment is downtown, so once I take the bus or bike to Oakland, I can't pop back to it for lunch. My fastest option for a complete (or semi-complete) meal is to go someplace on Pitt's campus, which takes 10–20 minutes for both directions of travel (and costs a nontrivial amount of money if it's a daily thing).

The next two factors are a bundled pair: having time to cook or prepare food, and having a kitchen in which to cook or prepare food. For most of the last six months, I haven't had either. I've touched on my limited time and limited access to my apartment during the day, but access to a kitchen (or even a refrigerator) hasn't been a constant either. For some of that time, I had a studio apartment with maybe half of the components of a functioning kitchen. For much of that time, I was sleeping on couches with no permanent residence. I have an apartment downtown now, but I don't yet have a lot of the things you need for a working kitchen. It's a fixable problem, but not without dipping into my money and time, which are resources that I'm already stretching thin to acquire the food I have now.

As for dietary restrictions, those also sap away at my available options. For years, I was vegetarian, for a mix of moral reasons. I've loosened that for now, out of a recognition that my survival is a higher priority than reducing my carbon footprint, but it still bottlenecks the number of things I can consume without accepting that I'm compromising on my beliefs. That bottleneck also limits the variety I can get in my dietary intake. Most restaurants and dining halls only have a handful of vegetarian options, which stacks poorly with reduced ability to cook my own food.

Finally, for food to be useful, I have to be able to eat it. In the last few months, my appetite has plummeted, and I'll quickly get nauseous when eating. The most likely explanation is the physiological impacts of stress. I've obviously been under a lot of stress lately due to the unjustified investigation I'm under, the immense workload from classes I can't attend office hours or SI for, and the uncertain outcome of the hearing (which might or might not force me to change the trajectory of my entire life, with no way for me to know until it happens). The physical impacts of stress hormones on your body are well-documented. Although this would only be a secondary factor compared to the others, having less of a physical urge to eat in the first place definitely worsens the impacts of a situation that restricts my access to food.

The cumulative impact of all of those factors is that I'm routinely eating a fraction of my usual dietary intake. I've lost weight, against my will. In March I was around 150 pounds (I remember because I stood on a friend's scale fully loaded down with hiking gear, then dropped all my bags so I could subtract and calculate how much they weighed). When I went to the hospital a couple weeks ago, I was 130 pounds at most. That's around 15% of myself, gone, stolen by CMU. Although I only sometimes feel physically hungry, thanks to the stress reducing my appetite, I feel it most on some nights when I'm trying to sleep. My thinness makes me shiver when it's cold. I used to wear T-shirts in the snow, and now 20 minutes at a train stop on an autumn day is unbearably cold. I have near-constant tiredness and brain fog. Focusing is difficult, even with my prescribed ADHD medicine. My short-term memory is worse. Most of the mass I've lost is muscle, so I tire faster when climbing hills on my bike, and I can't lift heavy things like I could just earlier this year. One of my closest friends told me my face looks different, noticeably thinner. Someone else said the same thing days later, independent of the first. Both times I had to concentrate to not cry, because my own face isn't mine anymore, CMU has taken that too.

CMU's actions have, in fact, limited my access to food.
