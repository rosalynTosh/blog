The revolution will look like a campus

Fri, Oct 30, 2026 · 19:45 · Pittsburgh

Recently, I've struggled to reconcile my usual optimism with the landscape that I'm seeing. The post-revolution world I dream of seems impossibly far away sometimes, with turnout to actions dwindling as tiredness sets in. Incremental change is torturously slow, and the people don't seem primed for revolution. But as I've talked about before, I see some of the most valuable revolutionary acts to be the creation of "bubbles" that block out the usual forces of policing and governance, giving us brief footholds. In this post I want to expand on that idea.

What is a campus?

Wikipedia summarizes its page on "campus" as "…the land and buildings of a college or university, or a company's office complex." A campus is usually a collection of buildings, although it could just be one building, or a collection of disconnected spaces spread across buildings shared for other purposes. In addition to the productive spaces that facilitate the campus's direct purpose, like classrooms or offices, it will usually also provide for some or all of the occupants' survival needs. Water fountains and restrooms are always present. Most university campuses have dormitories for students. Institutions of all types will often have some form of dining facility. Large campuses might have gyms, medical clinics, chapels, prayer rooms, libraries, public transit stations, cross-campus shuttles, cafés, grocery stores, athletic fields, or more.

A campus is often livable for long periods of time. While a corporate office campus is unlikely to include housing or intend to accommodate for overnight stays, those are the exception compared to colleges, hospitals, and military bases. A campus often provides a much higher standard of living than a city or suburb, due to its density and opportunities for socialization. Many have written about how much of the uniqueness of "the college experience" can actually just be attributed to the benefits of living in such a space.

Most definitions you'll find will associate campuses with institutions of some sort. In our current world, that's not surprising; it's rare for hundreds, thousands, or tens of thousands of people to organize themselves into a dense cluster of spaces optimized for a certain objective without some hierarchical structure overseeing them. But my idea with this post is that a campus without an institution is not only possible, but a promising direction for some sort of revolution.

The vision

Imagine living on something roughly shaped like a college campus. In a small footprint, you'd have densely packed living spaces (probably apartments rather than dorm-style or semi-suite rooms); gardens and green spaces; workspaces (offices, studios, workshops); restaurants or dining halls; libraries; water bottle fillers and restrooms; gyms; medical clinics; stores; and other spaces that facilitate survival, socialization, or advancement of human pursuits.

If the campus is relatively self-contained, its inputs and outputs can be discussed without worrying about how resources are distributed within. The inputs would likely include food (minus whatever can be grown within the space), water, energy (whether in the form of electricity or the fuels used to produce it), non-food grocery items, office and hobby supplies, construction materials for growth or repairs, medical supplies, and raw materials for anything produced within. The outputs (aside from trash, recycling, and sewage) would include money and labor to purchase the campus's inputs, and any outgoing mutual aid to surrounding communities.

The ability to abstract the inputs and outputs of the campus at large is powerful. If occupants have relatively little business to do with the outside world, the distribution of resources — including currencies used — in that outside world would not necessarily need to govern how they're distributed within the campus. Trust-based distribution of resources and labor is difficult to scale, but the boundaries of the campus provide a cell-like semi-permeable membrane that allows the people within it to act like a small, discrete system while still benefiting from trade with the rest of the world (and its capacity for specialized manufacturing, which no small isolated group of people could possibly replicate).

People within the campus would still work, probably about as much as they would otherwise. Some percentage of that work would be within the campus, doing whatever gardening and cottage industry is worth doing internally, and the rest of it would be in the outside, capitalist world. How the money from that labor flows into the campus might be a little difficult to work out, but it'd be reasonable for some of it to go into the campus's shared financial resources, and some of it to remain with the worker. The defining distinction between mutual aid and charity is that mutual aid builds infrastructure that gives us a systemic advantage in the fight against capitalism, while charity just moves money around. The campus, with its scale and sources of comparative advantage, would be among the strongest forms of mutual aid: able to spend the proportion of money that one person (or family) spends on basic survival in a manner far more efficient than the individual could, without middlemen like landlords and consumer-facing retailers that skim margins off of goods they buy wholesale.

Once a non-institutional campus like this is set up, it would provide a foothold for disruption of the status quo. Unlike an isolated commune, it would be in direct contact with a surrounding urban (or suburban) population, with inflow and outflow of people and information. It could (and should) fill the functions of a school, public co-working space, library, food pantry, hospital, and defensive outpost. Once multiple are formed, they'd be able to offer aid to one another, and basic specialization could start to occur (some things aren't worth buying two of if you know someone else who has one). Universities are already able to supplant most of the functions of local government for their students; a large, sophisticated non-institutional campus could do the same.

A certain means by which one could potentially acquire a campus

If you're sold on the vision, you might now be wondering how we kickstart this whole process. Building a bubble of a couple thousand community-minded people with its own land, buildings, and (limited) production capital might be easier than bringing a whole nation to the brink of revolution, but it still takes assets and organization that we don't currently have just sitting around. Luckily, we don't need to do all that work ourselves. Turns out, campuses are pretty easy to find!

Colleges and universities continue to be on the front lines of radical political organizing in the US. Colleges and universities also tend to come with beautiful, already-built campuses that students, staff, and faculty — the people — are thoroughly familiar with, more so than their admins (walled away in their towers as they are), and certainly more than local police and national guard. Many school campuses become so complex that first responders can't be trusted to navigate them under normal circumstances (prompting the creation of campus police and EMS services). You might call that an excuse for the slow creep of fascism into our most vulnerable spaces, but I call it a tactical advantage!

Unlike city blocks and suburbs, schools also have relatively high turnover and little sense of ownership. Students stay for four years or so, then they're expected to leave. Staff and faculty often move frequently as well. It's not anyone's permanent home, and whatever corporations are allowed to set up shop typically don't have as permanent of a presence as local businesses. If anarchists can take over a city neighborhood for days to weeks, that's good progress, but they're like lichen clinging to rocks. Flat surfaces and shallow buildings can be swept quickly, and forces will continue to desire the right of return for the petty bourgeoisie for decades to come. But a college campus is well-connected, deep physical space: it's hard to flush out people who have controlled the space long enough to fortify it and make it their own. We would be like pinecones sprouting in the blackened earth after a wildfire.

The ready-made campus approach would come with its own issues compared to the more sanctioned routes for building a non-institutional campus, of course. It might be harder to do business with companies on the outside, for example, and getting stable employment to provide the campus with income could be riskier. It would be more ideal if a large group could all put down their life savings and buy or build a cluster of buildings near a city, but if it was that easy to liberate ourselves from the unmitigated brunt of capitalism, more people would have done it. Us youths are more radical than our parents' generations, and a number of the US's colleges aren't contributing to the world quite as positively as one would hope, so it might in fact be time to toughen up a little and take a bolder approach.
